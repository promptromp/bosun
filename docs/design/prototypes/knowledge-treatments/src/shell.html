Knowledge home: four visualization treatments

bosun console prototype. All data is fictional ("Acme"). Self-contained: no network requests, no libraries.

Research summary. Good graph products follow Shneiderman's mantra (overview first, zoom and filter, details on demand) and fight the hairball by filtering, aggregating and ranking by centrality rather than drawing everything. They fade context instead of hiding it (Kumu focus/showcase), let the graph grow over time (Obsidian time-lapse, Ogma timelines), and offer task-specific lenses (Neo4j Bloom perspectives, Backstage kind/depth filters). Structural layouts beat force layouts when position should mean something (hive plots, hierarchical edge bundling, metaphorical metro maps), and motion supports meaning without carrying it alone (reduced motion).

10 applied ideas
  1. Overview, then zoom/filter, then details on demand. Every treatment opens on a whole-base overview; facts only appear in a panel on click. Shneiderman 1996
  2. Show less: rank and filter instead of drawing every edge. Size by connectedness, axis filter chips, labels only for important nodes. Cambridge Intelligence
  3. Fade, do not hide. Hover dims non-neighbors to ~15% so context stays readable. Kumu showcase/focus
  4. Time as a first-class filter. An "as of" slider replays how the base grew, like Obsidian's Animate and Ogma's temporal filtering. Obsidian, Ogma
  5. Task-specific lenses over one graph. Bloom perspectives and Backstage's kind/depth filters argue for several purpose-built views over one model, which is what the A-D switcher is. Neo4j Bloom, Backstage
  6. Position should mean something. Hive plots and radial hierarchies give stable, comparable layouts; force layouts do not. Krzywinski hive plots
  7. Bundle edges along the hierarchy. Route relations through type and axis rings to cut clutter. Holten 2006
  8. Processes read best as routes. Metro maps give lines, stations and interchanges for free, but the metaphor needs checking against the domain. Haverkort, metaphorical metro maps
  9. People, content, activity. Enterprise graphs that work pair entities with the activity around them, which is what the "this week" feed shows. Glean knowledge graph, Capacities typed objects
  10. Motion supports meaning, never carries it alone. All animation stops under reduced motion and state stays visible as text and shape. web.dev

Why

The most "alive" option and the closest to what people picture when they hear knowledge graph. Clusters by axis give an instant sense of where knowledge is thick or thin; the time slider shows the base growing; hover-dimming keeps 70+ nodes readable instead of a hairball.

Trade-offs

  • Positions carry no meaning beyond cluster; layout differs slightly per load.
  • Weak for ordered things (process steps) and for "what should I do today".
  • At 500+ entities it needs aggregation (collapse by type) to stay readable.

Production effort

Medium, 3-5 days. Canvas renderer, camera, hit-testing and the panel are hand-written here (~450 lines). Persist node positions so the map is stable across visits.

Library

Hand-written physics works at this size (this file). For robustness vendor d3-force as one file (~15 KB min / ~6 KB gz incl. quadtree for Barnes-Hut). Avoid force-graph (~150 KB+).

Why

Processes are the hardest knowledge to grasp from a fact list and the most valuable to an agent. A metro map makes order, gates and hand-offs readable at a glance, and interchanges show which systems tie processes together. The atlas covers People and Product with forms where size and grouping mean something.

Trade-offs

  • Automatic octilinear layout is hard; this mock uses a hand-tuned grid.
  • Lines only fit about 6-10 processes before a picker is needed.
  • Three panels compete for attention; less "single hero".

Production effort

High, 6-9 days. Auto-layout: one row per process, shared stations aligned to columns, simple octilinear router (as here). Treemap and team packing are ~120 lines each.

Library

Hand-write. Squarified treemap and packing are small; d3-hierarchy (~7 KB gz) is an option for packing. No library solves metro layout well.

Why

Answers "what changed and what needs me?" in five seconds, which is the real daily job of the home screen. The review stack turns the nearly-all-proposed base into a habit (confirm / skip / reject with keys), and superseded decisions read as before/after.

Trade-offs

  • Less spatial "wow"; the graph appears only as mini ego views.
  • Needs good fact sentence rendering (predicate templates).
  • Metrics like "rediscovery saved" need instrumentation we do not have yet.

Production effort

Low-medium, 3-4 days. Plain HTML/CSS plus tiny SVG; the review stack wires straight to the existing confirm/skip/reject API.

Library

None. Rings, sparklines and ego graphs are 10-30 lines of SVG each.

Why

Puts the user at the center and makes the axis/type/entity hierarchy literal, so position always means the same thing (stable across visits, unlike a force layout). Zoom-to-segment plus breadcrumbs gives clean drill-down; bundled edges show cross-axis relations only when asked.

Trade-offs

  • Arc lengths are hard to compare; labels crowd past ~120 entities per ring (zoom fixes it).
  • Says little about time or status.
  • Feels more analytical than inviting.

Production effort

Medium, 4-5 days. Partition layout, arc paths, zoom tween and B-spline bundling are hand-written here (~300 lines).

Library

Hand-write, or vendor d3-hierarchy + d3-shape (~15 KB gz together) for partition, arcs and curveBundle.
